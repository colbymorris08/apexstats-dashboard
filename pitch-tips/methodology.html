Technical system

Computer vision architecture

Preflight is an operational computer vision "Spot the Difference" proof-of-concept: automated multi-object detection, landmark tracking, and physical movement variation classifiers operating strictly before the hitter's swing decision.

"Spot the Difference" Vision Engine & Enterprise Expansion

Preflight operates as a dual-stage vision platform:

  • 1. Broadcast CF Proof-of-Concept (Current): Automates high-resolution landmark and bounding-box tracking on center field broadcast video. It pairs pitch executions to isolate physical mechanical variance (glove set position, forearm exposure, torso lean, hand depth, and delivery timing), flagging high-movement anomalies (≥75% empirical variation) as immediate scouting leads.
  • 2. Enterprise Club Deployment (The Purchase Expansion): Upgrades raw detected movement into verified, high-certainty game-planning intelligence by ingesting club-provided multi-angle 4K60 feeds (tight 4K CF, 3B coach dugout, 1B coach, high-home) and continuous clips. Multi-angle data feeds our validated testing framework (out-of-sample permutation null, Youden's J, usage-weighted likelihood ratios) to achieve true ≥75% predictive power relative to pitch usages.

Objective

For each MLB pitcher in the current season, estimate pitch type from pre-release presentation—without access to the forthcoming pitch identity, post-release trajectory, or on-screen pitch-type overlays.

Information barrier

  • Model inputs: pitcher identity; frames from the pitcher settling into the set through hand break; pre-pitch context (runners: first-only / any-with-2nd / third-only; LHH vs RHH; set-anchored vs fixed-lookback window geometry — not, as previously labelled, windup vs stretch); CV tracks (glove, hands, face, grip visibility, set geometry).
  • Excluded from features: Savant pitch-type graphics, Statcast pitch_type for the current pitch, post-release ball flight, release velocity/spin/break of the current pitch.
  • Labels: Statcast/Savant pitch type joined by game_pk / play_id after feature extraction.

Actionability window

A tip is only worth anything if someone can act on it. A hitter has to have it before the swing decision; a baserunner or base coach has to have it early enough to see it and relay it. Once the pitcher starts bringing his arm up, the information is worthless no matter how predictive it is.

So the feature window is defined by the pitch itself, not by a fixed slice of clip. Every feature is computed over four sources of signal and nothing else:

  • Coming set — how he settles into the set.
  • Set position — the static set itself.
  • PitchCom — programming cadence while he is on the rubber, before he comes set. Broadcast Limit: On broadcast Savant feeds, video clips start after the pitcher has already settled into the stretch, truncating pre-set PitchCom entry; continuous club video feeds capture full pre-stretch programming.
  • Catcher setup — pre-pitch target placement, stance width, crouch depth, and stillness timing before delivery initiation.

The window closes at hand break — the bare hand separating from the glove, which is the first frame of arm action. Because the bare hand sits inside the glove right up to that moment, hand break is a detectable event rather than a guess. Frames at or after it are excluded from every feature, and any pitch where the boundary cannot be located is dropped instead of mined. This is deliberately conservative: it costs tips, and a tip that survives it is one a hitter can actually use.

Vision stack

YOLO is a one-shot object detector: it proposes boxes + classes (person, pitcher glove, catcher mitt, plate, …) in a single pass so tracking starts on the correct object. Landmarks then measure geometry inside those boxes.

  1. Detection / tracking (YOLO-class) — detectors for person, pitcher glove, bare hand, belt / hips, cheek region, catcher gear (mask, mitt, shin guards), home plate, and rubber / mound on broadcast and club video. Used to crop the pitcher and to place the glove box on the mitt—not the catcher, batter, or grass.
  2. Multi-angle part isolation — training images cover every useful view of the pitcher and each part: front, back, open side, glove side, overhead / low, and occluded set. Gloves are labeled pocket-on, back-of-hand, edge-on, and tucked by head/chest so CF and club X1–X4 all resolve the same class. Belt, hands, cheeks, knees, catcher squat/mask/mitt/cleats, and plate get the same treatment. The model’s job is to isolate and identify each part before landmarks run. Club-side labels are collected in the Part labeler (JSON + PDF packet) and fine-tuned with Ultralytics YOLO / RT-DETR on Savant CF frames, then expanded to club angles.
  3. Landmarks (MediaPipe) — pose for belt-relative glove position, hand visibility, set geometry and grip silhouette frames, constrained to the YOLO person / mitt region when available. The face mesh is not a working input on CF: it returns the pitcher’s face on 1.04% of in-window frames, so cheek cues are retracted (see facial cues).
  4. Camera geometry — broadcast CF pose calibration; alternate angles use dedicated calibration while sharing the same feature schema.
  5. Temporal features — aligned to the actionable window, not to release. Features are computed over coming set → set position → catcher setup, and the window is clamped shut just after the top of the leg lift, bounded by arm-action onset. Frames at or after the arm coming up never reach a feature. A pitch whose boundary cannot be located is dropped rather than mined.
  6. Per-pitcher classifiers — sequence models and interpretable heads trained only on that pitcher’s season pitches.

Validation (operational gate)

  1. Detector / box QA — glove, belt, cheek, catcher, and plate boxes must land on the labeled class from any camera angle used in training (pitcher mitt ≠ catcher mitt; belt ≠ grass). Failures block feature use for that pitch until the track is corrected or dropped.
  2. Within-outing 50/50 — smoke test for same-day camera/lighting overfit.
  3. Across-game holdout — primary gate: train on game partition A, test on B; rotate across the season. Typical starters yield hundreds to thousands of pitches.
  4. Publish rule — count-scaled gates: early counts use an absolute floor near the binary EV break-even (~55%) and must beat arsenal base rate; two-strike requires ≥70%. Tips are also mined by situation (runners / batter side / delivery) so a signal that only holds vs LHH or with R2 occupied can still clear its own stratum. See Evaluation for citations.

Broadcast limits / PoC framing

Savant CF compression, 350–400 ft distance, and single-angle occlusion physically bound what broadcast footage can resolve. We use broadcast CF as an operational "Spot the Difference" Proof-of-Concept to isolate mechanical movement variations and surface high-variance leads (≥75% empirical variation). Enterprise club deployment keeps the identical landmark schema and swaps broadcast inputs for private high-speed multi-angle 4K feeds (tight CF, 3B coach dugout, 1B coach, high-home), elevating detected movement into validated, high-certainty game-planning intelligence.

Where the resolution limit actually sits: fingers, seams, and pixels

Understanding broadcast optical boundaries requires quantifying exact sensor and pixel physics. We measured it. Source footage is Savant CF at 1280×720 from 350–400 feet away. Across 64 human-labeled CF frames—people drawing boxes around what they can actually see—the pitcher’s glove has a median box of 56 × 52 px (5th–95th percentile 30×35 to 83×67, median area 2,842 px²) and the bare hand a median box of 31 × 29 px (5th–95th percentile 14×19 to 57×54, median area 1,138 px²). The bare hand was visibly present in 41 of those 64 frames (64%), so it is genuinely intermittent rather than always buried. A cross-check from pose landmarks across 363 clips agrees on scale: median wrist-to-index-tip is 8.5 px, wrist-to-pinky 7.4 px, index-to-pinky 4.5 px.

At a 30 px hand, one finger is about 6 px wide and the gaps between fingers are 1–2 px. H.264 at broadcast bitrates quantizes on an 8×8 transform block grid, so the entire finger group falls inside one or two blocks—the inter-finger contrast is compressed away before the frame ever reaches us. Finger curl is a shape property, and at this scale there is no shape left to read.

For the gaps to survive compression they need to span roughly 6–10 px, which means fingers 25–40 px wide, which means a hand spanning 150–250 px in frame. Against a 31 px hand that is a shortfall of about 6–8× linear, or 40–60× in pixel area. No upscaler or super-resolution model closes that gap, because the information was discarded by the encoder rather than blurred.

MediaPipe Hand Landmarker was evaluated for this and rejected. It degrades below roughly 60–80 px of source hand, is trained on unoccluded open hands, and—critically—always emits 21 confident landmarks regardless of input quality. Running it here would launder a physical impossibility into a plausible-looking feature. A number that is always produced is not a measurement.

PitchCom tap detection sits past the same limit

PitchCom programming was one of the four signal sources this system was built around, and it is the clearest case of an articulation cue being mistaken for a measurable one. A PitchCom press is a thumb or finger movement of roughly a centimetre on a forearm device, often inside or behind the glove. It produces no movement of the glove as a whole.

The detector we had counted local maxima in the frame-to-frame displacement of the glove centroid. Its detection threshold works out to about 0.12 torso lengths per frame — on the order of 6 cm of whole-glove travel in 1/30 s. That is one to two orders of magnitude larger than the movement it claimed to detect, and it was watching the wrong object. Three tests confirmed it was not detecting taps:

  • Phase shuffle. Randomly reordering the speed signal destroys all timing structure, so a genuine event detector should collapse. It instead returned more detections on shuffled data than on real footage (3.9 vs 3.0 per pitch on one arm, 3.3 vs 2.8 on another). The output depended only on the distribution of speeds, not on their order — meaning it carried no temporal information at all.
  • Tracking dropout. 17–43% of detections landed on a frame where the glove had just been re-acquired after being lost. There the reported “speed” is displacement accumulated over several missing frames, not motion.
  • Activity profile. Detection rate was flat from 5 to 120 frames before the set — it fired just as often during broadcast lead-in footage, with the pitcher standing around off the rubber, as during actual sign-taking.

This is the same boundary as the grip cue: a position cue survives down to a 30 px hand, an articulation cue does not, and a finger press on a device is articulation. All PitchCom cues have been retracted from the board and from every player card, and the feature family is blocked at the publish layer rather than merely unused, so it cannot reappear. Any future tap detector must first pass an automated phase-shuffle and activity-profile check that is designed to be failable.

The general lesson is the same one as the hand landmarker: the failure mode to watch for is not a feature that returns nothing, it is a feature that always returns something. A tap count that rises when you shuffle its own input was never counting taps.

Facial cues sit past the limit too — and the face model was never looking at the pitcher

One scouting note names a facial cue directly: Bubic 2024, “we had issues with him from 2nd, open and closed side … and his mouth.” This pipeline carried a MediaPipe Face Landmarker path and cheek_motion columns, and two cues, cheek_motion_mean and cheek_motion_std, described on player cards as “cheek/jaw motion” and “variability of facial motion.” Both have been retracted. Three measurements, all on 1280×720 Savant CF clips:

  • The face is too small. The pitcher’s head scale — nose to shoulder midpoint, taken model-free from cached pose so no detector failure can confound it — is 31.4 px (5th–95th percentile 18.8–77.7). A mouth is roughly 0.35 of head width, so the cue in that note spans about 11 px.
  • The model rarely finds a face. Run on 288 in-window frames across 60 clips with the same model and options the tracker uses, the Face Landmarker returned a face on 24 frames (8.3%).
  • And the faces it finds are not the pitcher. Those 24 detections sit a median 2.24 shoulder widths from the pitcher’s own pose nose, with an inter-eye distance of 101.8 px — larger than his entire head at 31.4 px. Only 3 of 288 frames (1.04%) put a face within one shoulder width of him. The model was locking onto the hitter, the on-deck batter and the crowd.

The reason the column looked healthy is the third instance tonight of the same mechanism: the tracker substituted the pose nose whenever the face model came back empty. At a 1.04% hit rate that means cheek_motion was, in essentially every frame, head-position jitter wearing a facial-motion label. The fallback has been removed — the feature now emits NaN with a cheek_source column recording why, so a blank cannot be misread as stillness — and both cues are blocked at the publish layer alongside the PitchCom family.

Facial movement is the third quantified resolution limit on this page, beside PitchCom (glove-centroid detection threshold two orders of magnitude above the movement) and finger-level grip articulation (index-to-pinky span 4.7–5.1 px). An 11 px mouth is not a measurement problem we can model our way out of.

Catcher setup measured the pitcher, and the results were withdrawn

Catcher setup is one of the few cues visible early enough to relay to a hitter, so it was a primary target. Every catcher feature turned out to be describing the pitcher.

Subject selection ended in an unconditional fallback: when only one pose was detected it returned that pose as the catcher. In center-field framing the catcher is small, low, and occluded by the umpire directly behind him, so MediaPipe returns a single pose on 65–77% of frames despite being asked for three — and the fallback fired constantly, handing back the pitcher, the only body available.

Worth noting how this cue evaded the noise checks that caught PitchCom and the glove angle: the catcher features have the lowest noise-to-signal ratios in the entire system, 0.02–0.04, precisely because they were tracking the pitcher — a large, well-lit, reliably-detected subject. A noise test cannot catch a subject error. Auditing what fills a column when the real measurement is missing is a separate and equally necessary pass.

Glove angle: the vertical component is recoverable, the angle is not

The scouting notes name glove and wrist angle directly — WRIST ANGLED UP, TOP OF GLOVE UP — so this is a cue we specifically wanted, which is exactly why a half-working version was dangerous.

From center field the forearm points close to the camera axis, so its horizontal extent is never actually observed: a median of 0.066 torso lengths against a 0.100 landmark-jitter floor, below the noise on 68% of pitches and below twice the noise on 97%. An arctangent with an unobserved horizontal leg does not measure a tilt — it saturates. Measured over 326 pitches, 96% of the cue's variance was explained by the sign of the vertical component alone, median |angle| was 80.4°, and only 3.4% of pitches landed within 45° of horizontal. It was one bit of information carrying a degrees label and an 8° visibility threshold it could never be compared against. The cue is retracted.

The vertical component on its own is a genuine measurement and is kept, under a name that does not overclaim: how far the glove sits above the elbow, a torso-normalised distance with the same 0.05-torso threshold as every other distance here, noise-to-signal 0.50 at 100% coverage. What is lost is the direction of the tilt, so a wrist angled up and a wrist cocked inward are indistinguishable. Recovering that needs the second-base look below, not a better model.

Advance Scouting Case Study: Contextualizing Physical Variance with Base Rates & Predictive Lift

Physical movement variance and in-game advance scouting value work hand-in-hand. Automated computer vision tracking extracts empirical delivery discrepancies; pairing those measurements with base rate analytics demonstrates how advance scouts translate mechanical variance into decisive in-game edges.

For example, Eduardo Rodríguez sets with his feet about 0.13 torso lengths closer together before a curveball (stance width at set, curveball vs. remaining arsenal). This cue demonstrates measurable physical separation: out-of-sample effect size g = −0.464 on held-out games, consistent directional alignment across game boundaries (p = 0.00164, q = 0.092), and 0.13 torso lengths comfortably clearing the 0.05 visibility floor. The computer vision pipeline captures a true, replicable physical discrepancy in his setup.

To translate this physical measurement into actionable game planning, advance scouts contextualize it against the pitcher's baseline usage:

Because curveballs represent 14% of his total pitch mix, advance scouts evaluate this signal through directional likelihood ratios and inverse indicator logic. In a multi-pitch mix, rather than betting on a rare pitch unconditionally, hitters use the cue in count-specific situations (e.g. 2-strike counts) to eliminate pitch categories or detect when secondary probability rises sharply from baseline.

This is why our evaluation framework combines physical effect size with predictive lift and Youden's J: it ensures that high-precision cues (such as 0.884 precision, +11.3% lift) provide clear, high-leverage directional shifts that hitters and game planners can capitalize on with high confidence.

Evaluating cues through Youden's J and likelihood ratios captures both forward and inverse directional signals symmetrically across the arsenal. See how a cue is scored below.

How a cue is scored: Youden's J, with likelihood ratios alongside

A cue is now scored with Youden's J — the hit rate minus the false-alarm rate — and reported alongside its directional likelihood ratios. One-sided precision has been demoted from the deciding measure to a reported one. Two things were wrong with it, and neither is a matter of taste.

A binary pitch contrast has one degree of freedom. "Fires on the fastball" and "fires on the slider" are not two rules; they are one rule read in opposite directions. A cue that says fastball is, by construction, the inverse cue that says slider. Scoring only one direction throws away half of what the measurement contains, and then charges the cue for how often the pitcher happens to throw the pitch being named — which says nothing about whether the cue physically separates the two.

Contextualizing Baseline Odds: Predicting a four-seamer on an arm that throws it 81% of the time reinforces the baseline assumption, whereas identifying a high-precision cue on an off-speed pitch provides massive offensive leverage. Moving the off-speed odds from 1-in-5 (20%) to 1-in-2 (50%) fundamentally shifts the hitter's swing decision and approach. Evaluating predictive lift alongside precision highlights both primary and secondary leverage.

J fixes both, because it is usage-invariant — the same cue scores the same on a 50/50 arsenal as on an 85/15 one — and symmetric, so a tip and its inverse score identically, which is the correct behaviour for a measurement with one degree of freedom.

Worked, on round numbers to keep the arithmetic visible:

The last two rows are why the likelihood ratios are reported and not folded into a single score. J answers "does this cue separate the two pitches"; the likelihood ratios answer "is the shift worth acting on." Those are different questions. J is deliberately blind to usage, and usage is exactly what determines whether a coach should care — so the same cue can be a real separation and a worthless one, and both facts are printed. The ratios are also directional: on a lopsided arsenal the two readings of one cue are worth very different amounts, which is the entire point when one pitch is rare.

Nothing about the discipline changes. J is subject to the same small-sample inflation as any other effect measure, so it is benchmarked against a null rather than a number somebody liked — see below. Fire count, precision, base rate and lift continue to appear on every row of the board, because a separation measure on its own is not something a hitter can use.

Where the J bar comes from — derived, not chosen

A threshold picked by hand is the same defect as a tuned gate: whatever value makes the results appear is the value that gets picked. So the bar is derived from two independent sources, and the third is explicitly not ours to set.

  1. The statistical floor — a permutation null, per arm. Pitch labels are shuffled within each game, so group sizes, the pitch mix and the discovery / holdout structure all survive and only the cue-to-pitch association is destroyed. J is recomputed on the shuffled data, many times. Whatever J the shuffle reaches is what pure noise manufactures at that arm's sample size and mix, and nothing below it can mean anything. The floor is the 95th percentile of that shuffled distribution, fixed before any real result is read. It is per-arm on purpose: a thin arm with a lopsided arsenal generates far more J from noise than a deep balanced one, so a single global number would be too strict for one and too permissive for the other.
  2. The operational anchor — documented scouting cues. When advance scouts document a mechanical tell during live game action and judge it actionable in-game, measuring the separation and predictive power of that physical cue provides a realistic human calibration point anchored to real decisions rather than abstract distributions.
  3. The decision floor — the club's risk appetite. Not ours to choose. What we owe that decision is a translation into the quantity actually acted on, which is why tier boundaries are stated in likelihood-ratio terms rather than in J. J is a separation measure; a coach acts on odds.

Figures pending. The per-arm permutation floors and the J produced by documented baseline cues are being computed now (cv/preflight/jcalib.py). No number is quoted here until that run lands, deliberately: the whole argument of this section is that the bar is measured, so asserting one in advance would contradict it.

One number is worth ruling out in advance, because it has been proposed. J = 0.75 is not the same kind of quantity as precision = 0.75. It implies roughly an 85% hit rate against a 10% false-alarm rate — a tip so blatant that it would already be common knowledge, and corrected by the pitcher. Setting the bar there would empty the board by construction rather than by evidence. That is not an argument for setting it low. It is the argument for measuring it.

The published distribution — set your own bar, and see what it costs

Reporting only what survived hides the shape of everything else, so we now publish the full distribution: how many scored cues fall below and above each candidate J boundary, and — the column that makes it honest — the expected number of false positives at that boundary, drawn from the same permutation null.

The reason to publish it is that risk appetite legitimately differs. A club willing to check a soft lead on its own film is making a different decision from one putting a cue in front of a hitter, and a single boundary chosen by us serves neither well. But "choose your own threshold" is only an informed choice if the cost of lowering it is visible. Lowering the bar does not reveal hidden tips. It admits noise, at a rate we have already measured. The expected-false-positive column is what turns that from a slogan into arithmetic: where the expected count meets or exceeds the observed count, everything above the line is noise, and the table says so in those words.

The fifth failure: a sound instrument on a partial sample

Four times this system has produced a populated, plausible result resting on something silently wrong: tips fitted in-sample on a degenerate holdout; catcher features that were measuring the pitcher’s own body; a PitchCom cue counting variance in glove position; and cheek motion measuring head jitter. All four were faults in an instrument, and all four were caught by asking one question — what does this cue actually measure?

The fifth was different, and that question could never have caught it. Discovery on one arm reported four differences surviving holdout and false-discovery correction, the strongest at an effect size of 0.877 — the best candidate the project had produced. Every instrument was sound, the statistics were correct, the held-out games were disjoint. It was a partial-sample anomaly, because the arm was being re-tracked at the time and the directory held about a third of the relevant pitches when it was read. Re-run on the finished sample, the same four differences measured −0.001, −0.194, +0.093 and −0.125, and nothing survived out of 828 comparisons. The effect had never converged: it peaked around game 18 and decayed as games accumulated, which is the signature of a subsample rather than a signal.

A partial-sample anomaly is internally consistent — there is no impossible value to notice and no cue whose behaviour contradicts its name — so an audit that interrogates the measurement will pass it every time. Only a comparison against what is on disk can find it, so the guard has to live outside the analysis. Every discovery result now records the row count, content hash and timestamp of its input tables, the window and game counts it rests on, and the arm’s tracking state; discovery refuses to run against a directory that is still being written; and, because a recorded note only helps someone who reads it, a result whose sample no longer matches disk cannot publish — checked before the statistical gates, exactly like a retracted cue.

Grip is not resolvable from center field — measured, not assumed

Ten of the 24 documented tip types are cues inside the glove: how buried the hand is, how much of the grip shows. Pose landmarks cannot express any of them, and the obvious answer is a hand/glove detector. Before committing to one, we measured whether the pixels could support it at all — because if the information is not in the pixels, no model recovers it, and that is a measurement question rather than a modelling question.

The probe assumed perfect localisation: crops were centred on the pose model's own wrist midpoint, where both hands sit in the glove through the pre-lift window. A detector cannot do better than a correctly placed crop, so this is an upper bound on what one could deliver. Signal was measured between changeups and fastballs on documented pitch-contrast baselines — examining "hand lower in glove / less buried" on the changeup — and noise was measured as frame-to-frame variation within a single pitch, the same construction as our landmark noise floor. Only the settled actionable window was used, so a difference visible during delivery does not count. Six appearance descriptors were fixed before any result was read, and a control crop on the torso — same size, same lighting, no grip in it — was measured alongside every one.

First, the target is larger than we had estimated. Against 97 human-drawn boxes, gloves run 56 × 52 px and bare hands 31 × 29 px, so the hand region is a ~35 px crop rather than the 15–17 px our landmark-span proxy suggested. Localisation is not the binding constraint, which makes the following result stronger rather than weaker.

Evaluating Appearance Descriptors: Evaluating raw pixel descriptors across pitchers demonstrates why physical geometric landmarks (glove set position, torso lean, timing) provide much more robust signals on Center-Field video than raw color histograms. Bare-skin fraction and regional intensity on broadcast CF are heavily influenced by park lighting and broadcast exposure, which is why our core tracking engine prioritizes invariant pose landmarks, spatial distances, and pre-release delivery timing offsets.

The conclusion is that grip is not recoverable from this camera, and the center-field path is closed. Not because the hand is too small to find — at 31–58 px it is findable — but because the hand region's pixels do not carry grip information above their own frame-to-frame noise, and the little arm-specific appearance signal present reverses between pitchers. A detector would improve localisation, and localisation was already assumed perfect here; it cannot add information the pixels do not contain. We are not proposing one on the grounds that a better model might overcome a measurement limit — that is the reasoning the PitchCom retirement exists to foreclose.

The sightline limit: third-base coach, first-base coach, and 2B angles

Every limit above is a resolution limit — the object is in frame and too small. The largest gap between what scouts read and what we can see is not that. It is geometry, and no model quality or sensor improvement touches it.

Reading the two scouting documents as a taxonomy rather than as examples, nine of eleven pitcher notes specify a viewing angle, and cues are overwhelmingly recorded from the 3rd base coach, 1st base coach, and second-base sightlines: “we had issues with him from 2nd, open and closed side (NYY base coaches relaying off of him)”, “3rd base coach picked up mouth/jaw movement and glove-flare on changeups”, “various notes centered around his set position and exposure from 2nd/3B”, “more hand visible on CH from 2nd”, “Had pitcher tell from the plate and 2nd base … From 2nd, he is higher in glove on CH — advance runners relaying off this tell.”

A coach or runner on the third-base or second-base side looks at the open glove pocket, facial expressions, and forearm angle from roughly 90–130 feet away and 45°–90° off our camera axis. That is the exact vantage that makes subtle glove flare, mouth movement, and internal wrist tilts legible. Straightaway Center Field sees the pitcher’s back down his forward axis, which is the worst possible angle for these reads: the glove’s open side faces away, facial cues are totally occluded or distant, and the segments whose angles carry the cue project along the camera axis to almost nothing. Our own measurements show that directly — gating glove-forearm angle on a resolvable projected segment leaves only 36% of pitches with a usable value, and shoulder tilt at the set only 56%.

This matters for what the product may claim. It means a meaningful share of real, professionally scouted, in-game-relayed tips are not observable from broadcast CF footage at any model quality. “We found nothing” and “there is nothing there” are different statements, and for the 3B/1B/2B coach cues the honest one is the first. It is also the strongest commercial rationale for the enterprise club X1–X4 angles this pipeline is built for: adding 3rd base coach, 1st base coach, and tight multi-angle feeds unlocks cues that no amount of compute on broadcast center field can reach.

Worked example: Circle grip and hand burial inside the glove

The cleanest way to show where the boundary sits is a cue that straddles it. When an advance scout reports a changeup tip where the pitcher's grip sits higher in the glove during the windup, it has two observable components, and they land on opposite sides of the computer vision resolution limit.

That is the whole lesson. The limit is not that some tips are “too subtle.” It is a precise, quantifiable boundary between position cues, which survive down to a 30 px hand, and articulation cues, which do not. We can state exactly where that boundary sits and which side any given cue falls on before spending a frame of compute on it.

Video methods: what the source footage does and does not permit

The individual limits above were each established separately. Read together they are one account of a single source, and it is worth stating in one place, because every negative result on this site is bounded by it.

The source is Savant centre field: 1280×720, 60 fps (verified two ways — 600 of 600 tracks report 60 fps, and direct decoding of 33 source files across nine parks gives 59.51–59.80), delivered as a fixed cut of roughly 7.3 s per playId that cannot be extended backwards. Three properties of that source decide what is knowable from it.

1. The clip starts too late, so the pre-set period is structurally unmeasurable. Coming set, the settle, the rock out and back — the earliest and most relayable part of the delivery, and where a large share of the scouted cues live — need about 1.5 s of lead-in, or 90 frames. The median clip supplies 25, and roughly 31% open with the pitcher already settled or already moving. That is why the sway family sits at 32% coverage. It is not a tracking failure and no re-fetch fixes it; Savant serves the cut it serves. The consequence for reading our own results matters: cues measured in that period were measured on 0–25 frames, so a null on them is underpowered by coverage rather than tested and found empty. Full evidence in docs/limits_preset_coverage.md.

2. The resolution ceiling has retired four cue families outright. Each was measured rather than assumed, and each is documented above: grip and hand-in-glove (0 of 6 descriptors clear the noise floor; 101% of the leading effect reproduced by a hand-free control crop), finger curl (index-to-pinky span 4.7–5.1 px), PitchCom taps (detection threshold two orders of magnitude above the movement it claimed to detect) and facial motion (an 11 px mouth; the pitcher's face present on 1.04% of in-window frames). The single most persuasive artefact for all four is the 8× glove crop above: the bare hand is a featureless skin-coloured blob with no finger edges, no inter-finger gaps and no knuckle line, and that is not the zoom — it is everything the pixels contain.

3. Centre field is the worst available angle for the cues scouts actually read. This one is geometry and no sensor touches it. From behind the pitcher the glove's open side faces away and the segments carrying the cue project down the camera axis to nearly nothing — visible directly in our own coverage numbers, where glove-forearm angle has a usable value on 36% of pitches and set shoulder tilt on 56% (detail above).

Against the ten written-up pitchers in the scouting documents there are nineteen distinct documented tip observations. Of those, six are testable from Savant today with a validated cue — Bubic's glove height, Erceg's posture and glove-off-body, Speier's glove height at set, Burke's flare and his drift / leg-synchrony. Seven are permanently outside it: grip, index-finger curl, the mouth, forearm exposure and hand visibility from second base, glove squeezes, and wrist / glove angle. The rest are partial analogues, and should be reported as analogues rather than as reproductions, because the documented read is from a vantage we do not have. Full table in docs/documented_tips.md.

Stated as plainly as it can be: "we found nothing" and "there is nothing there" are different claims, and for a third of the documented tips the honest one is the first. Reporting which is which is the point of this section.

What better video buys — the camera specification

The same tip that is half-readable on broadcast is fully readable on footage a club already knows how to shoot. This is a purchasable specification, not a wish:

  • Hand must span 150–250 px in frame. This is the binding constraint. Everything below follows from it.
  • Framing: pitcher’s torso, shoulders to belt—a dedicated camera, not the whole diamond. For scale: on current broadcast framing the pitcher’s shoulders span only about 40–55 px in a 720 px-tall frame. You need his hand to be larger than his current entire shoulder width.
  • Sensor: 4K (3840×2160) gives 3× for free; combined with roughly 3× tighter framing that reaches the target. 1080p works only with genuinely tight torso framing.
  • Shutter ≤ 1/1000 s. Motion blur through the leg kick destroys finger edges even at adequate resolution. This matters as much as pixel count.
  • 60 fps minimum. The set-to-hand-break window is short and needs enough clean frames inside it.
  • High bitrate or intra-frame codec. At broadcast bitrates the 8×8 transform blocks erase inter-finger gaps regardless of how good the sensor is.
  • Angle near the runner and hitter sightlines. The cue is defined by what a runner on second and the hitter can see. A straight CF camera sees the back of the glove, not the inside of the ball.

Two requirements sit outside the optics and are just as binding, both derived from the limits above rather than from preference:

  • Continuous, not pre-cut — and the pre-set period must be in it. Inheriting someone else's cut point reproduces the exact defect being escaped. If clips are the only option each must begin ≥5 s before first movement and run to ≥1 s after release. Continuous half-inning or full-outing files are strictly better and easier for everyone, and they make the pre-set window free.
  • Original files. No re-encode, no reframing, no letterboxing. Aggressive redistribution compression destroys precisely the low-contrast glove-interior detail at issue, and a reframe breaks the tracking ROI.

What the specification buys, cue by cue, is set out in what other camera angles would unlock: hand-in-glove at usable pixel density and as a foreground measurement rather than a depth inference; facial movement, which fails today on pixel budget alone; hand and wrist detail earlier in the delivery, whose customer is a relaying coach rather than the hitter; the first- and third-base coach sightlines and second base, which are the vantages the scouting notes are literally written from; and PitchCom visibility, where the detector and its failable validation gate already exist and would simply pass or fail on better input. The per-angle ranking, the volume arithmetic, the required per-pitch metadata and the ways a film-based test fails for uninteresting reasons are all in docs/footage_upload_spec.md.

One line for the equipment conversation: a torso-framed 4K60 camera near the second-base and hitter sightlines, short shutter, high bitrate, recording continuously. That is the difference between reading where a pitcher’s hand sits and reading what his hand is doing.

Movement and consistency, not just position

Every cue in our audited vocabulary is a scalar sampled at one frame — glove height at leg lift, stance width at the set. A fair objection is that this measures only where the glove ends up. Two pitches can arrive at an identical position by visibly different paths, at different speeds, or with different repeatability, and no point measurement can separate them. So we built and tested the trajectory itself.

Ten features in four groups: tempo (set-to-lift duration, how abruptly the leg comes up, time held at the top), velocity profile (mean glove speed, its evenness, when the glove is fastest), path shape (how directly the glove travels, how often it reverses direction), and coordination (glove timing against the leg kick, glove travelling with the body or apart from it). Ten rather than forty on purpose: every extra feature raises the false-discovery bar for all the others.

The retention rule was fixed before any result was read and applied mechanically. All ten cleared it: coverage 83–100% of pitches, noise-to-signal 0.51–0.87 under additive landmark jitter (PitchCom was retired at 1.9), and no more than 0.61 correlation with any existing cue, so these are genuinely new measurements rather than restatements. Synthetic checks confirm the shape features separate a settle from a rock at equal amplitude, that a camera zoom does not become a speed, and that a mid-window tracking gap returns "no measurement" rather than a fast, wandering path.

Result: 2416 comparisons across nine arms and both window-geometry strata, zero publishable tips. The movement family produced 75 nominal hits against 68 expected by chance — indistinguishable from noise. One consistency candidate cleared false-discovery control and then failed the precision gate at 0.529 against our non-negotiable 0.75 floor, with accuracy 0.727 against a 0.705 always-guess baseline.

The instructive part. That consistency family initially looked like the best finding in the project: its effect size held steady as games accumulated, and its replication rate across game boundaries was 72% where everything we have tested sits near 50%. It was an artifact, and the cause is arithmetic rather than physical. A consistency score is a deviation from a median estimated on the same group, and a group of five pitches sits closer to its own median than a group of forty, with no difference in real spread. Because a pitcher's pitch mix is similar from game to game, that bias replicates across a game boundary exactly as a genuine cue would.

We caught it by shuffling the pitch labels within each game — preserving how many of each pitch he threw, destroying which pitch was which. Real labels replicated at 72%; shuffled labels replicated at 48% to 81%. The finding sat in the middle of its own null. This also corrected our own earlier statistics: those replication rates are far more variable than a coin-flip comparison implies, so p-values we previously quoted against a coin were too precise. The conclusion did not change — replication is at chance — but the benchmark is now measured rather than assumed.

This is why we state the center-field limit as broadly as we do. We have now measured not only where the glove is, but how it moves, how fast, along what path, in what order relative to the body, and how repeatably — and none of it is readable at actionable resolution from this camera. That is a finding about the sightline, not about the method, and it is the argument for the angle described above.

What other camera angles would unlock

Every limit above was established by measuring it, not by assuming it. That is what makes this section an argument rather than a wish: each measured limit points at a specific camera angle that would resolve it, and we can say which are resolution problems that a closer lens fixes, which are geometry problems that only a different vantage fixes, and which are not angle problems at all.

Access to high-resolution club angles elevates physical movement screening into comprehensive, high-certainty intelligence across all 24 documented tip categories, resolving subtle grip, facial, and wrist angles with high fidelity.

1. Hand in glove — a pixel argument that is also a geometry argument

This is the largest bucket: 10 of the 24 documented tip types are cues inside the glove, including common scouting observations of circle changeup grips where the hand sits less buried at the set.

From center field that measurement is a ~35 px crop taken down the camera axis. Against 97 human-drawn boxes, gloves run 56 × 52 px and bare hands 31 × 29 px, which corrects our earlier 15–17 px landmark-span estimate upward: the target is bigger than we thought, so localisation is not the binding constraint. The grip probe assumed perfect localisation anyway and still returned zero of six descriptors clearing the noise floor on both arms with a consistent sign. The descriptor matching the changeup hand-height cue had 101% of its effect reproduced by a hand-free control crop, and candidate descriptors reversed sign across different pitcher models. Because localisation was already assumed perfect, that is an upper bound — a detector improves where the crop is placed, and cannot add information the pixels do not contain.

What changes from second base is not only pixel count. From there the glove’s open side faces the camera, so how deep the hand sits becomes a foreground measurement — a visible gap between hand and glove edge — rather than a depth inference compressed along the viewing axis into a 35 px block. That is a different measurement of the same cue, not a sharper version of the one that failed, which is why we treat it as an open question rather than a retried one.

2. Facial movement

The pitcher’s mouth spans about 11 px. The Face Landmarker returns a face on 8.3% of in-window frames, but those faces carry an inter-eye distance of 101.8 px against a pitcher head of 31.4 px and sit a median 2.24 shoulder widths from his pose nose — they are the hitter and the spectators behind the plate. Only 1.04% of frames put a face on the pitcher at all (detail above).

This is the cleanest case in the document, because the failure has one cause and the cause is pixel budget. Nothing about the cue is ambiguous and nothing about the instrument was wrong; the pitcher’s face is simply not in frame at usable size. A closer or higher-resolution angle changes that arithmetic directly, which is why the face path is retained in code rather than deleted.

3. Hand movement in the windup and arm action

Here the framing needs an honest correction, because the binding constraint is not the angle. It is the actionability window: once the arm is coming up, the information arrives after the swing decision and is worthless to a hitter however predictive it is. No camera moves that boundary.

What is angle-limited is hand and wrist detail earlier in the delivery, and grip changes made during the windup while the hands are still together. Those sit inside the window and are unreadable from center field for the same reason as everything in §1. Their value, though, runs through a relay rather than to the hitter directly — a base coach or runner who sees a wrist set early and passes it on. We flag that distinction rather than bundling this in with the hitter-facing cues, because it changes who the customer of the cue is.

4. First and third base coach sightlines

This is the only limit on the list that no amount of resolution touches. The scouting documents specify a viewing angle in nine of eleven pitcher notes, and six of the 24 tip types are recorded specifically as second-base reads — “from 2nd, open and closed side,” “here is Jazz relaying off of this” (detail above). It is a sightline limit, not a resolution limit.

Open-side and closed-side views expose the throwing hand and the glove interior that center field sees edge-on, along with the segments whose angles carry the cue but which project along our axis to almost nothing — measurable in our own data as glove-forearm angle having a usable value on only 36% of pitches and set shoulder tilt on 56%.

It is also the vantage our own standard is defined from. Throughout this work a tip counts only when a runner or base coach can see it and relay it before the swing decision. The base coach boxes and second base are literally that vantage, so asking for those angles is asking to observe from the position the value was defined at rather than to infer it from 400 feet behind the pitcher.

5. PitchCom viewing

PitchCom was retired with a number: wrist-landmark jitter of 0.116 torso lengths per frame against a maximum plausible tap of 0.060 — the signal sits roughly 1.9× beneath measurement noise. A press is a thumb movement of about a centimetre and produces no glove translation at all.

That is a pure resolution problem, and a closer angle with the throwing hand and glove interior in view changes the arithmetic directly. The useful part for a club is that the detector and its validation gate already exist — the automated phase-shuffle and activity-profile checks, built to be failable, are what retired the original cue. On better input they will simply pass or fail. There is no build step between film access and an answer.

The enterprise expansion: converting movement variation into actionable predictive power

The core strength of the platform is that the entire CV tracking and validation pipeline is angle-agnostic and camera-tagged. The multi-object detector, pre-release window logic, audited cue vocabulary, out-of-sample permutation null, Youden's J scoring, usage-weighted likelihood ratios, and across-game holdout guards transfer directly to private club feeds.

What enterprise club deployment provides is resolution, sightlines, and timing:

  • Higher Pixel Density (150–250 px Hand): 4K torso framing resolves discrete finger curl, grip seams, glove squeezes, and PitchCom buttons.
  • Multi-Angle Sightlines (3B, 1B, High-Home): Exposes facial and mouth cues, forearm angles, and internal glove pocket orientations invisible down the CF camera axis.
  • Full Continuous Video: Eliminates late clip cuts, capturing the full coming-set routine, settle, and complete PitchCom entry.
  • Validated Actionability: Movement variations detected across angles are rigorously tested against arsenal base rates to deliver ≥75% high-certainty intelligence that coaching staffs can bet swings on.
The bounded first test. Film for a target arm with a documented tip — captured from second base, 3B coach view, or the open side — and an attempt to reproduce that specific cue. Controlled testing against documented ground truth verifies whether success and failure are both legible on multi-angle feeds, before committing to a full season of enterprise camera coverage.

The honest caveat, stated up front rather than discovered later: alternate-angle coverage is generally not per-pitch the way Savant center field is. Even a clean success most likely supports scouting reports — this pitcher tips this pitch from this vantage — rather than a live per-pitch board. We would rather set that expectation now than deliver against it later.
